## 1. Define Ventures — recent activity

**The firm.** ~$800M AUM across six funds; SF-based; exclusively healthcare/digital health;
founded by Lynne Chou O'Keefe (ex-Kleiner Perkins). Initial checks ~$1–5M, incubation through
Series B. Portfolio includes Hims & Hers, Cohere Health, Unite Us, Tia, FOLX Health, Laudio,
Layer Health, Luminai, 9amHealth.

**Fund news.** Closed **$460M across two vehicles** — Fund III (incubation → Series B in new
companies) and an Opportunities Fund (growth follow-on into existing portfolio). This roughly
doubles their deployable capital and, importantly, means they can now *defend* winners rather than
get diluted out.

**Recent deals:**

| Company | Date | Round | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| **9amHealth** | May 2026 | **$26M Series B, Define led** | Virtual specialty care for cardiometabolic conditions (obesity, diabetes, hypertension, cholesterol). Now expanding beyond cardiometabolic into the chronic conditions that drive up to 70% of employer pharmacy spend. Claims >$50M projected medical + pharmacy savings across two Fortune 100 employers. Lynne Chou O'Keefe took a board seat. Co-investors: SemperVirens, Catalio, NewHealth Ventures. |
| **Blank Bio** | May/Jun 2026 | **$7.2M seed (participated)** | Applied AI research lab training **RNA foundation models** on tumor transcriptomes for precision oncology — predictive/prognostic biomarkers, patient trajectory modeling, clinical diagnostics. | **Layer Health** | Dec 2025 | (participated) | Clinical data abstraction / LLMs over the medical record. |
| **Hyro** | Oct 2025 | (participated) | Conversational AI for patient access and health system call centers. |

**Stated thesis — the "House of Healthcare."** Define published an AI investing thesis built on four
layers: (1) front-door personalization, (2) data foundation, (3) insight extraction, (4) workflow
automation. The framing: foundation/walls/hallways = turning raw data into AI-ready data; the
"rooms" = where AI touches the point of care (clinical decision support, AI-developed drugs, scribes,
automated prior auth). Two explicit filters they've articulated:

1. **Multi-product founders.** They want teams with a pipeline of products deployable across
   *multiple teams inside one buyer* — not a point solution.
2. **Systemwide transformation, not isolated use cases**, with demonstrable cost savings, because
   provider economics are compounding badly.

**Talent motion worth noting:** Define runs an **AI Fellows** program — an 8-week free program for
prospective healthcare-AI founders, with intros to buyers and C-suite operators. This is a
founder-sourcing funnel, and a useful place to look for pre-seed companies 6–12 months before they
show up in a database.

## 2. Y Combinator — recent batches

**Batch shape (2026).** Batches are running ~190–235 companies. Roughly **60% AI** (up from ~40% in
2024), ~64% B2B. Healthcare holds at **8–9% of each batch** — 21 healthcare companies in S26 out of
234. The consistent editorial read across batches: AI stopped being a feature and became the
substrate, and founders have tilted hard toward the **physical world and regulated industries**
(robotics, energy, agriculture, aerospace, defense, healthcare) rather than another chat wrapper.

- **Atlas Discovery** — AI-native pharma company. Foundation models of patient drug response
  trained on pre-clinical + clinical data, with autonomous research agents. Their framing is the

  **$150M in expected revenue a program never gets funded** — and drug development cost is set by
  the knowledge work required, not by market size.*

  Claims $50M+ in annualized national insurance contracts, 600+ clinicians across 70 locations in
  32 states, and 8,000+ independent clinicians (1,000+ practices, 300,000+ patients) running their
  practice on Allia's system of record. Care delivery starting September 2026.
- **TareBio** — reads out immune cells already fighting a disease and reverse-engineers the
  antigens that activate them; claims a vaccine that cured cancer in mice; pitch is
  personalized *or* population-level vaccines in weeks.
- **Gamgee** — end-to-end platform for **personalized mRNA cancer vaccines for dogs**.
- **Hubble** — AI-powered **medical records retrieval API** for developers; team came out of AI

## 3. What's circulating publicly (the LinkedIn-feed proxy)

**Macro.** U.S. digital health funding hit **$7.4B across 244 deals in H1 2026**, ~$1B ahead of
H1 2025 — a clear multi-year recovery. But **mega-deals ($100M+) took 45% of all capital across
just 8% of transactions.** Rock Health has stopped treating "uses AI" as a differentiator at all;
they now point to four durable moats: **founder edge, operating-layer scale, hands-on delivery
(forward-deployed engineers), and compounding network effects.** Mental health was the top-funded
indication for the seventh straight year, with weight management/obesity right behind it on GLP-1
and consumer peptide demand. 115 M&A deals closed in H1; Q2 2026 was the busiest acquisition
quarter since 2021.

**Rounds people have been posting about:**

- **OpenEvidence** — ~$250M Series D at a **$12B valuation** (Jan 2026); walked $1B → $3.5B → $6B →
  $12B across successive rounds; clinical literature synthesis, reportedly used by ~40% of US
  physicians.
- **Abridge** — $300M Series E (a16z, Khosla) plus a reported ~$316M extension in 2026;
  **$5.3B valuation**, $100M+ ARR, live in 250+ health systems.
- **Ambience Healthcare** — $243M at $1.25B; expanding from documentation into coding and
  broader clinical workflow.
- **Qualified Health** — **$125M** to scale enterprise AI *governance/deployment* inside health
  systems (SignalFire among investors).
- **Assort Health** — $120M Series C at $1.2B (Menlo), explicitly repositioning from voice AI to an
  "agentic operating system for the patient journey."
- **Pearl Health** — $110M debt + equity; AI agents for Medicare provider administration.
- **Superluminal Medicines** — $60M Series B (BVF, Deep Track, Perceptive, RA Capital, Insight,
  **NVIDIA**, Catalio, **Eli Lilly**).
- **Elucid** — $55M Series D (Sept 2026), AI cardiovascular imaging/diagnostics.
- Mid-market: **Voize** $50M (AI companion for nurses), **Hellocare.ai** $47M, **Happy Health**
  $75M, **Arintra** $25M (autonomous medical coding), **HealthSnap** $25M, **Nabla** $24M.

**The pattern to hold onto:** capital is concentrating in companies that **own a workflow and are
accountable for a result**, not companies that add an AI feature. Clinical AI took ~$1.47B (~35%)
and care-workflow AI ~$1.31B (~31%) of healthcare AI funding.

## 4. Ideas — what these deals open up

Ordered roughly by conviction. Each has the thesis, the specific evidence from Section 1–3, and
what would have to be true.

### 4.1 The accountability layer *after* the scribe

**Thesis.** Ambient documentation is done as a venture category — Abridge at $5.3B and Ambience at
$1.25B have taken it, and Ambience is already migrating into coding. Value now sits downstream: the
note becomes a claim, the claim becomes a denial, the denial becomes recovered or lost dollars. YC
funded three shots at this in one batch (**ClaimGlide**, **Overdrive Health**, plus **Arintra**'s
$25M outside YC), which is a market signal, not a crowding problem — none of them owns the outcome.

**The opening.** An RCM company that is **priced on recovered dollars, not seats.** Health systems
cannot underwrite another SaaS seat license; they can underwrite a contingency fee. This also maps
exactly to Define's "multi-product, multi-team" filter — coding, denials, appeals, and prior auth
are four products sold into one CFO.

**What has to be true:** the company must be willing to look like a services business early
(forward-deployed engineers — one of Rock Health's four moats) and hold gross margin above ~60% as
it scales. Watch for margin decay masked by ARR growth.

---

### 4.2 Payer-side evidence, and the agent-to-agent adjudication layer

**Thesis.** OpenEvidence built a $12B business giving *physicians* synthesized evidence. There is no
equivalent on the *payer* side, and yet the payer is where the argument is actually settled.
Meanwhile both sides are automating into each other: Define's **Cohere Health** automates prior
auth for payers; YC's **ClaimGlide** automates prior auth *against* payers. Within 18 months, a
material share of prior-auth traffic will be one model arguing with another model.

**The opening.** Two companies here:

1. **Payer-grade evidence synthesis** — medical-necessity criteria, coverage policy, and literature,
   with an auditable citation trail a utilization-management nurse can sign. Buyer: health plans and
   the UM outsourcers.
2. **The adjudication substrate** — a neutral protocol/clearinghouse where provider agents and payer
   agents exchange structured, evidence-backed requests, with **gold-carding** priced off measured
   accuracy. Whoever sits in the middle sees both sides' data and earns per-transaction economics.

**What has to be true:** you need one large payer and one large system live on the same rails.
This is a two-sided cold start, which is why it's undervalued — and why it should probably be
incubated (Define's model) rather than found.

---

### 4.3 Governance, provenance and liability infrastructure for clinical agents

**Thesis.** **Qualified Health's $125M** is the tell: health systems are no longer buying one AI
tool, they're deploying dozens and discovering they cannot answer basic questions — which model
wrote this note, what version, on what data, who signed it, can we defend it in a malpractice suit
or an FDA inspection. YC funded **Decawork** to do exactly this control-plane job for IT, in a
domain with no HIPAA, no state medical board, and no product-liability exposure.

**The opening.** The regulated-industry version: model provenance, immutable audit logs, evaluation
harnesses tied to clinical outcome, drift detection against a **Predetermined Change Control Plan**,
and an incident pipeline that produces regulator-ready artifacts. Buyers: the Chief AI Officer role
that health systems have spent 2025–2026 creating, plus their malpractice carriers — an insurer
that can price agent risk will *require* this instrumentation, which is a much better distribution
wedge than selling to IT.

**What has to be true:** it must not be a dashboard. It has to sit in the inference path.

---

### 4.4 The long-tail drug program — and the regulatory bottleneck it creates

**Thesis.** Atlas Discovery's framing is the highest-leverage claim in the whole S26 batch: drug
development cost is set by knowledge work, and below ~$150M expected revenue nothing gets funded.
If agents cut that knowledge work materially, thousands of indications cross the economic
threshold at once. But **discovery was never the only fixed cost** — trials, CMC, safety reporting,
and regulatory submission don't fall just because the target-ID step did.

**The opening — pick the bottleneck, not the discovery layer:**

- **AI-native CRO for sub-$150M indications.** Trial-in-a-box for rare and neglected disease:
  protocol design, site/patient identification, synthetic or external control arms, regulatory-grade
  evidence packaging. The current CRO cost structure assumes big pharma budgets; nobody has rebuilt
  it for a 40-patient trial.
- **Pharmacovigilance at agent scale** *(highest-conviction pick for you specifically, given the
  SAE demo)*. Ten times more programs means a volume shock in individual case safety reports,
  E2B submission, signal detection, and literature monitoring — a workflow that is still substantially
  manual, offshore, and priced per case. An AI-native PV company that owns ICSR intake → coding
  (MedDRA) → causality assessment → submission, with a quality guarantee, sells into exactly the
  pain that a long-tail pipeline explosion creates. It is also the rare healthcare-AI market with a
  clean, auditable ground truth and a regulator-defined output format.
- **Regulatory intelligence as an operating system**, not a search box: continuous monitoring of
  guidance, precedent approvals, advisory-committee outcomes and label changes, wired directly into
  submission drafting. This is the natural commercial form of the regulatory-intelligence work
  already prototyped in this repo.

**What has to be true:** for PV, that pharma will let an AI system be accountable for a regulated
submission. Start with literature monitoring and case triage (lower stakes, high volume), earn the
submission.

### 4.5 The reimbursement path for algorithmic diagnostics

**Thesis.** Blank Bio (RNA foundation models + PacBio) and Elucid ($55M, AI cardiovascular imaging)
are two instances of the same pattern: models are producing clinically actionable outputs faster
than the payment system can absorb them. Every AI diagnostics company hits the same wall — CPT/PLA
coding, Medicare coverage determination, payer contracting, and the evidence generation required
to get there. Each company solves it once, painfully, in-house, with expensive consultants.

**The opening.** "Reimbursement-as-a-service" for algorithmic diagnostics and AI-enabled devices:
evidence-dossier construction, coverage strategy, code applications, and payer negotiation — sold
to the 200+ AI dx companies that will need it in the next three years. Boring, unglamorous,
enormous attach rate, and it accumulates the single most valuable proprietary dataset in the
category: *what actually got covered, by whom, on what evidence.* That dataset is the real company.

**What has to be true:** it must productize into software rather than stay a consultancy. Underwrite
the founder's ability to resist the services gravity well.

---

### 4.6 The AI-native clinically integrated group — replicated into other fragmented specialties

**Thesis.** Allia Health is the sharpest structural bet in S26: instead of selling software to
8,000 independent mental-health clinicians, aggregate them into one clinically integrated group,
put them all on one intelligent system of record, and capture the payer contract. Software margin
plus provider-group revenue, and it directly matches two YC RFS categories (AI-Native Service
Companies, The AI Operating System for Companies).

**The opening.** The template generalizes to every fragmented, insurance-reimbursed,
low-capital-intensity specialty where the incumbent roll-up is private equity with no technology:
**physical therapy, optometry, dermatology, audiology, home health, dental** (YC already funded
Patientdesk.ai on the software-only side), **fertility, and independent primary care**.

**What has to be true — and this is where most of these will die:** the moment you take clinical
risk you are a healthcare services company, valued at services multiples, with services capital
intensity. The bet only works if the AI system of record is what generates the margin expansion,
and if that expansion shows up in a same-store-sales metric within 12 months. Ask for cohort-level
contribution margin by clinic vintage. If they can't produce it, it's a PE roll-up wearing a
software costume.

### 4.7 Medical-record liquidity as a toll road

**Thesis.** YC's **Hubble** (records retrieval API) plus **Docura Health** (medical-legal
documentation) point at the same underserved primitive. Every agent being funded right now — prior
auth, billing, disability, life underwriting, legal, care coordination — needs the same input: a
complete, normalized, consented longitudinal record. Today each one builds its own retrieval spaghetti
against portals, faxes, and HIEs.

**The opening.** The retrieval + normalization + consent layer sold as infrastructure, priced per
record. Adjacent and possibly better: **consent and identity for record release** — the
authorization primitive that agents will need in order to act on a patient's behalf at all.

**What has to be true:** that TEFCA and QHIN participation commoditize the *access* but not the
*normalization*. If access becomes free and structured, this compresses to a thin API. Diligence
that question hard.

---

### 4.8 What happens after GLP-1s

**Thesis.** 9amHealth (Define-led) is explicitly expanding past cardiometabolic toward the
conditions driving up to **70% of employer pharmacy spend**, and Rock Health has weight management
as the #2 funded indication on the back of GLP-1s and the consumer peptide ecosystem. The entire
category is currently built on *initiation*. Almost nothing is built on what comes next.

**The opening — three under-built adjacencies:**

1. **Step-down and maintenance.** A large cohort is coming off GLP-1s (cost, coverage loss, side
   effects, supply). Weight regain and lean-mass loss are predictable and currently unmanaged.
   Whoever owns the taper protocol owns a cohort measured in millions, at far lower drug cost.
2. **Underwriting the savings claim.** 9amHealth cites >$50M in projected savings for two Fortune
   100 employers. Employers increasingly want that *guaranteed*, not projected. There is a real
   business in being the actuarial layer that prices and warrants pharmacy-savings claims across
   vendors — effectively a captive/stop-loss product for digital health performance guarantees.
3. **Consumer peptide safety.** The compounded/consumer peptide market is growing faster than any
   safety infrastructure around it. This connects straight back to §4.4 — an adverse-event and
   quality-signal layer for a market regulators are about to pay much closer attention to.

---

### 4.9 Two contrarian, cheaper shots

- **Veterinary as the fast-cycle clinical market.** Gamgee (personalized mRNA cancer vaccines for
  dogs) is not a joke deal. Vet medicine is cash-pay, has a lighter regulatory path, has real and
  rising willingness to pay, and lets a platform generate clinical evidence years faster than the
  human path. The generalizable thesis: **any platform whose bottleneck is regulatory cycle time
  should consider proving itself in animal health first.** Look for human-medicine platform teams
  who haven't yet realized vet is the cheapest possible Phase 0.
- **Clinical labor as a capacity market.** Define holds **Laudio**; **Voize** raised $50M for an AI
  nurse companion; YC funded **MochaCare** for care-agency scheduling and hiring. Everyone is
  building tools *for* the existing workforce. Nobody is building the market that clears clinical
  capacity across institutions — dynamic float pools, credential portability, cross-system
  per-shift pricing. Uber-for-nurses failed in 2016 because credentialing and trust weren't
  solvable; agents plus verified credentials change that input.

## 5. Filters I'd apply to all of the above

1. **Does it own a result, or add a feature?** Rock Health has explicitly retired "uses AI" as a
   differentiator. Ask what the company is *contractually accountable* for.
2. **Is it multi-product into one buyer?** This is Define's stated filter and it is a good one —
   healthcare sales cycles are too expensive to amortize across a single SKU.
3. **Which of the four moats?** Founder edge, operating-layer scale, forward-deployed delivery,
   network effects. Fewer than one and it's a feature.
4. **Where does it sit when the model layer commoditizes?** Scribes went from novel to table stakes
   in ~24 months. Assume any pure-model advantage has an 18-month half-life; underwrite the data
   asset, the workflow lock, or the regulated position instead.
5. **Beware the concentration trap.** 45% of capital went to 8% of deals. At seed, that means the
   bar for a Series A has moved *up*, not down. Ask what the Series A story is at $3–5M ARR, and
   whether the round after this one has an obvious lead.
